This task asks you to design and build the core of a real system, and then to document and defend your design. **The design document matters at least as much as the code.** Please read this in full before you start.

## The Problem

Many websites want to run experiments: show different versions of a page to different visitors, measure which version performs better, and personalise what each visitor sees. Imagine a lightweight service that any website can call, from a small JavaScript snippet on the page, to answer one question on every page load:

**"Which variant should this visitor see right now?"**

and later to record what happened, so the service can report which variant is winning.

Your task is to design and build the core of that service.

Note that this service sits on the **critical path of page rendering**. Every visit to every customer site calls it. That constraint should shape much of your thinking.

---

## What to Build

Build and deploy a working service that covers the core of this system. At minimum:

### 1. Assignment
An API that, given a visitor and one or more active experiments, returns the variant that visitor should see. It must be:
- **Deterministic and sticky.** The same visitor must always receive the same variant for a given experiment, across repeated calls and across server restarts. Think carefully about how you achieve this and what you do and do not need to store.
- **Respectful of traffic allocation.** An experiment might be split 50/50, or 90/10, or hold back a percentage of visitors as a control. Your assignment must honour these allocations correctly across the visitor population.
- **Able to handle multiple experiments and multiple variants** at once.
- **Fast.** It is on the page-render path.

### 2. Tracking
Endpoints to record that a visitor was **exposed** to a variant, and later that they **converted** (reached some goal). This data must be stored durably, and it should be resilient to obvious problems such as duplicate exposure events.

### 3. Results
A dashboard page or an endpoint that reports, per experiment, the exposures, conversions, and conversion rate for each variant.

### 4. Configuration
A way to define an experiment: its variants and its traffic allocation. An API is fine; a minimal UI is welcome but not required.

### 5. An AI-assisted element
Support variants whose content (for example, a headline or a call to action) is **generated by an LLM**. You decide where in the system this belongs. Part of what we are assessing here is your judgment about **where an LLM call should and should not sit**, given that the assignment path is latency-sensitive and LLM calls are slow, costly, and can fail. Keep the API call on the backend and never expose keys.

### Deployment
The service and its storage must be **deployed and live**, reachable from our machines, not only running locally. You may use any stack, any database, and any host. A small demo (a test page or script that exercises the API) is a plus.

## The Design Document

This is a core deliverable, not an afterthought. In the repository, include a design document that covers:

- **Architecture.** How the pieces fit together, and the key decisions you made, each with the reasoning behind it.
- **Determinism.** How you guarantee sticky, well-distributed assignment, and why your approach works across the whole visitor population.
- **Scale.** How this behaves at millions of assignment calls. Where the bottlenecks are, what you would cache, and how read traffic and write traffic scale differently.
- **Reliability and failure modes.** This is on the page-render critical path. What happens when your storage is slow or unavailable, or the service itself is degraded? How do you fail safe so that you never break a customer's page? What is the default behaviour when things go wrong?
- **Correctness.** How you keep assignment accurate and counting trustworthy, including how you think about the statistical validity of the results you report.
- **The LLM decision.** Where you placed the LLM work and why, and how you handle its latency, cost, and failure.
- **Trade-offs and next steps.** What you deliberately did not build, what you would do with more time, and how you would extend the system (for example, smarter allocation than a fixed split, or carrying learnings across many sites).

## Constraints

- **Any stack.** Use whatever you are most effective with. We have no preference.
- **Deploy everything.** Service and storage must be live and hosted.
- **Push to GitHub.** In a repository we can access, public or private with an invite.
- **Do not mention any company.** Our company name and product must not appear anywhere in the code, commits, README, repository name, or the service. Keep it entirely generic.
- **AI coding tools are allowed and encouraged.** Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot, or anything similar. We use these ourselves. The one condition is that you understand and can defend everything you ship.

## How We Will Evaluate It

- **Quality of design and decisions.** The heart of this task. Are your choices sound, and do you justify them well?
- **Determinism and correctness.** Does your assignment actually behave correctly and consistently across the population and across restarts?
- **Fail-safe thinking.** Do you treat the critical-path nature of the service seriously?
- **Scale and cost reasoning.** Do you understand where this breaks and what it costs as it grows?
- **Judgment on the LLM placement.** Did you put it in a sensible place and reason about its constraints?
- **Code quality and Git hygiene.** Clear, well-organised code and meaningful commits.
- **The design document.** Can we understand your system and your reasoning from it?
